Buying Privately on Finance: What Happens on Which Day

People who ask how to finance a car from a private seller usually want one thing answered: how long will it take, and what do I have to do first. The honest reply is that the calendar depends on two people, you and the owner, and that most of the delay in a private purchase is caused by doing sensible things in the wrong order.

What follows is the order we suggest. It is a working plan and not a promise, because no broker controls how quickly a seller returns a call. But buyers who follow it tend to collect their car while others are still waiting for a settlement letter.

Stage one: before you view the car

Do the finance groundwork before you fall for the car. That means a conversation with a broker about budget, deposit and structure, and indicative terms in writing. Across our lender panel the indicative band on current cars is 8.9 to 9.9 per cent, deposits are usually 10 to 20 per cent and terms run from 24 to 60 months. There is no minimum advance. Knowing where you sit inside those ranges means you can negotiate like a cash buyer.

Then check the car on paper. DVLA guidance on GOV.UK says to ask the seller for the registration number, make, model and MOT test number before you travel, and to compare them with the official record. Any car more than 3 years old needs an MOT every year, so there should be a continuous test history showing mileage rising steadily. Gaps and reversals are questions to ask on the phone, not on the driveway.

Stage two: the viewing and the handshake

At the car, ask to see the V5C. Check the name and address against the seller’s identification, check the vehicle identification number on the document against the one on the car, and hold the log book to the light for the DVL watermark. These are the same checks a lender will repeat later, so anything that troubles you now will trouble the underwriter as well.

If you want the car, agree the price in two parts: subject to finance, and subject to checks. Put it in a message so both sides have a record. Tell the seller plainly that a finance company will contact them, that it pays them directly, and that it will want identification, the log book, bank details and a signed invoice. If the seller has an agreement of their own on the car, ask them to request a settlement figure that day.

A holding deposit is a matter between you and the seller. If one is paid, keep it small, pay it by bank transfer to the person named on the V5C, and get the terms for its return in writing.

Stage three: from handshake to approval

Now the full application goes in. The lender will want your details, the car’s details and the agreed price. On an individual application expect proof of identity, address and income. On a company application expect accounts and details of the directors.

The valuation happens here too. A lender advances against what the car is worth, and on a rare or heavily optioned car that can differ from what was agreed. If the valuation is lower, the gap is normally bridged with a larger deposit or a conversation with the seller about price. Finding this out early is one more reason to agree the price subject to finance.

While that runs, the seller sends their documents. In our experience this is the step that sets the pace. A seller who has their passport, a utility bill, the V5C and a bank statement to hand can complete their part in an afternoon.

Stage four: completion day

Completion follows a strict sequence. You sign the agreement. You pay your deposit in the way the lender directs. The lender transfers its advance to the seller, having first paid off any existing funder. The seller confirms receipt. Only then do you collect.

Before you drive away, three things have to be in place. The car must be insured in your name. It must be taxed, because vehicle tax does not transfer with a car: when the seller tells DVLA about the sale their tax is cancelled and they are refunded any full months remaining. And you need the green new keeper slip from the log book, which is what lets you tax the car on the spot.

A worked example of the monthly cost

Suppose the car is agreed at £165,000 and you put down £33,000. The lender advances £132,000 to the seller. On hire purchase over 48 months at 9.9 per cent the payment is about £3,342 a month. If you would rather defer part of the price, a lease purchase with an agreed final payment of £60,000 brings the monthly figure down to about £2,318, at the cost of more interest overall and a lump sum to deal with at the end. The £60,000 is a figure chosen to show the arithmetic and is not a forecast of what any car will be worth.

Representative example only. Rates vary by individual circumstances. This is not a formal offer of finance.

What usually goes wrong

Four things account for nearly every stalled private purchase. The seller is slow with documents. The log book is in a different name from the bank account. The seller’s settlement figure runs out before the funds are sent. Or the buyer finds the car first and arranges the money second, so the seller has already lost patience.

None of these is about credit. All of them are about sequence, which is why the plan above front loads the dull parts.
